6. Conclusions

6.1 Summary of Findings

This study investigated the Capital Dilution Effect (CDE)—the hypothesis that altcoin proliferation systematically erodes Bitcoin's market dominance by dispersing investment capital across an expanding universe of competing assets. Our empirical analysis of 2013-2025 data yields five principal findings:

  1. Dominance is cyclical, not eroding steadily: Bitcoin dominance averaged 93.2% in 2013 and stood at 59.3% on 1 November 2025, but its record low was 32.4% (14 January 2018) and it gained 22 percentage points between the 2022 trough and 2025. Share loss is real; a permanent structural dilution by token proliferation is not what the data show.
  2. The pool of capital is not fixed: Total market capitalisation grew roughly 2,300-fold from April 2013 to November 2025, and the two series move together as often as they move apart: in 2021–2022 capitalisation and dominance both fell, and in 2023–2025 both rose. The bounded-pool premise behind the effect does not survive contact with the data.
  3. Proliferation explains little of dominance: Tracked assets rose from 2,213 (May 2019) to a peak of 10,884 (February 2025) before falling to 9,386, but the association with dominance is weak once trends are removed (r = −0.29 on month-over-month changes, about 8% of variance). Earlier drafts of this paper claimed r = −0.92; that figure was withdrawn.
  4. Market cycles drive the share loss: It clusters in 2017 (−23.5 pp) and 2021 (−14.1 pp); recovery clusters in 2019 (+15.2), 2023–2025 (+6 to +7 per year). This is the part of the hypothesis the data support.
  5. The share loss is concentrated, not diffuse: What Bitcoin lost between 2021 and 2025 went to a handful of large assets rather than the long tail: Ethereum's dominance peaked at 22.0% (December 2021) and is 12.6% now, and DeFi TVL peaked at $174bn and is $144bn.

6.2 Theoretical Implications

The equity-dilution analogy does not survive the move to a permissionless market, and the way it fails is the paper's main contribution:

First, equity dilution is arithmetic because issuance is bounded by the size of the company; token creation is bounded by nothing, and the capital available to the sector is not fixed either. The two properties that make the equity case mechanical — a fixed pie and a fixed share per new unit — are both absent, which is why the correlation between asset count and Bitcoin's share collapses once trends are removed.

Second, market share in crypto is allocated among a small number of assets, not across the population of assets. The share held outside the seven largest coins fell from 21.1% in May 2019 to 9.8% in November 2025 while the tracked count quadrupled; whatever moves Bitcoin's share is decided in a market of a few names plus stablecoins, and stablecoins (0.4% in 2018, 7–14% since 2022) are the closest thing the sector has to cash.

Third, the one robust pattern is cyclical: dominance compresses during speculative expansions and recovers afterwards, with a roughly four-year period. That is a statement about risk appetite, not about token supply, and it is what a model of the sector should be built on.

6.3 Policy Implications

Our analysis supports a minimal intervention regulatory framework based on the following reasoning:

  • Market self-regulation is effective: High project failure rates (CoinGecko counted 52.7% of tokens listed since 2021 as no longer trading), cyclical bear market purges, and institutional quality filtering keep the dilutive segment small without government intervention.
  • Innovation preservation is critical: Permissionless token creation drives blockchain innovation; regulatory barriers risk stifling beneficial experimentation.
  • Regulatory arbitrage is inevitable: Attempts to restrict token creation in one jurisdiction simply drive projects to more permissive jurisdictions, rendering regulation ineffective while concentrating activity outside regulatory reach.

We recommend governments focus on:

  1. Fraud prosecution: Aggressive enforcement against scams, rug pulls, and fraudulent schemes
  2. Taxation clarity: Clear rules on capital gains, income, and reporting requirements
  3. Consumer education: Public awareness campaigns about cryptocurrency risks
  4. Systemic risk monitoring: Surveillance of stablecoin reserves and leverage in the system

Governments should avoid: (a) token registration mandates, (b) arbitrary distinction between "securities" and "utilities," (c) restrictions on permissionless smart contract deployment, and (d) attempts to "protect investors" from voluntary risk-taking in transparent markets.

6.4 Bitcoin's Long-Term Store of Value Thesis

Does the Capital Dilution Effect invalidate Bitcoin's store of value proposition? Our analysis suggests no, but with important caveats:

Bitcoin's store of value thesis remains viable because:

  • Flight-to-quality dynamics consistently reconcentrate capital in Bitcoin during risk-off periods
  • User adoption remains concentrated in Bitcoin: Crypto.com counted 337m Bitcoin owners out of 659m crypto owners in 2024 (51%)
  • Institutional quality filtering ensures Bitcoin captures majority share of regulated product inflows
  • Network effects, brand recognition, and liquidity depth create substantial moats against displacement

However, Bitcoin faces persistent challenges:

  • Market dominance will likely never return to 2013-2016 levels (80-95%)
  • Ethereum and a handful of large alternatives will keep commanding meaningful share
  • Stablecoins now absorb 7–14% of the market and behave like the sector's cash, which caps how much of a flight to quality ends up in Bitcoin
  • DeFi and L2 ecosystems create structural demand for non-Bitcoin native tokens

6.5 Final Verdict

The hypothesis that altcoin proliferation dilutes Bitcoin's market share is not supported. The share loss that did happen (93.2% in 2013 to 59.3% in November 2025) is cyclical and compositional: it moves between Bitcoin, Ethereum and stablecoins inside a market that grew roughly 2,300-fold, and it reversed for three straight years after 2022. The long tail — the part the hypothesis is about — shrank from 21.1% to 9.8% over the window in which its asset count quadrupled.

What investors and policymakers should expect, if this reading is right:

  • Continued BTC.D volatility in the 40-65% range, set by risk appetite rather than by token supply
  • Periodic alt seasons that compress dominance and then give it back
  • A top tier of a few assets plus stablecoins that keeps absorbing share from the long tail
  • Ongoing natural selection removing most new token launches (52.7% of tokens listed since 2021 have already stopped trading, per CoinGecko)

In conclusion, Bitcoin's store of value thesis is untouched by this test — it was never really tested here — but its dominance is better described as a share of risk appetite than as the residue of dilution. The original answer survives only in a weaker form: Bitcoin remains the sector's primary reserve asset, while the mechanism that was supposed to erode it does not appear in the data.
