4. Empirical Analysis and Results

All figures in this section are computed from two public series, cut off at 1 November 2025: CoinMarketCap's daily global metrics (BTC and ETH dominance, total market capitalisation, number of tracked assets) and DefiLlama's aggregate DeFi total value locked. The dataset and the script that builds it are in data/build_data.py; the numbers below are reproducible by running it. Where a source does not publish a series, we say so instead of reconstructing one.

Key Finding 1: Bitcoin dominance has fallen over thirteen years, from a 93.2% annual average in 2013 to 59.3% on 1 November 2025 — but through two complete cycles, not a trend. Its record low was 32.4% on 14 January 2018, and from the 2022 trough (37.6%) it recovered 22 percentage points in three years. Any claim that dominance is being eroded continuously has to explain why 2021–2025 ran the other way.

4.2 The pool of capital is not bounded

The Capital Dilution Effect assumes a roughly fixed amount of money available to crypto, so that every new asset takes a slice from Bitcoin. Figure 2 tests that assumption directly.

Figure 2: Total cryptocurrency market capitalisation, month-end (2013-2025)
Source: CoinMarketCap global metrics, month-end values, logarithmic scale.
Key Finding 2: Total crypto market capitalisation rose from $1.6bn on 29 April 2013 to $3.69trn on 1 November 2025, roughly 2,300-fold. More importantly, the two series do not move in opposite directions the way a fixed pool requires: between November 2021 and November 2022 capitalisation fell 68% ($2.61trn to $0.83trn) and dominance fell (41.8% to 37.9%); between December 2022 and September 2025 capitalisation rose 4.9-fold and dominance rose 18 points. Money enters and leaves the asset class; it does not only rotate inside it. The bounded-pool premise is not supported by the data the paper is building on.

4.3 Asset proliferation, and the contraction after it

CoinMarketCap only publishes a consistent count of tracked assets from 2019 onwards; earlier annual counts exist in archived pages but are not comparable. We therefore report the published series rather than the “50 → 17,000” figures in earlier drafts, which no source supports.

  • 2,213 tracked assets in May 2019.
  • 10,884 in February 2025 — the series peak.
  • 9,386 on 1 November 2025, a decline of 14% from the peak.
Key Finding 3: The proliferation is real — a 4.9× rise to the February 2025 peak in six years — but it stopped in early 2025 and has since reversed. For a 2025–2026 reader, “exponential proliferation” is the wrong description of a market that is delisting assets faster than it lists them.

4.4 Does the number of assets explain dominance? A weak, mostly spurious association

Figure 3 shows the two monthly series side by side, and the caption reports the correlations computed by the build script.

Figure 3: Tracked assets and Bitcoin dominance, monthly (May 2019 – Nov 2025)
n = 79 months. Correlation of levels: r = −0.54 (p < 0.001). Correlation of month-over-month changes (Δlog assets vs Δdominance): r = −0.29 (p = 0.010). Neither series is stationary, so the level correlation mixes a shared trend with any real relationship.
Key Finding 4: The association in levels is strong enough to look convincing and weak enough to be uninformative: once both series are differenced, so that only co-movement remains, the correlation drops to −0.29 and explains about 8% of the variance. The widely quoted “r = −0.92” does not reproduce from any series in this dataset.

Key Finding 5: The capital that left Bitcoin between 2021 and 2025 did not disperse across thousands of assets. Ethereum's dominance peaked at 22.0% on 9 December 2021 and is 12.6% on 1 November 2025; DeFi TVL peaked at $174bn in November 2021 and is $144bn now. What Bitcoin lost, it lost to a small number of large assets — a shift of a few percentage points, not a dilution across the long tail.

4.6 Year-over-year changes and the cycle

Figure 5: Change in annual average Bitcoin dominance
Percentage points, year over year. Negative bars are years in which Bitcoin lost share.

Dilution clusters in 2017 (−23.5 pp), 2018 (−14.0) and 2021 (−14.1). Recovery clusters in 2019 (+15.2), 2023 (+6.3), 2024 (+7.0) and 2025 (+6.1). The pattern is a cycle with roughly four-year amplitude, which is the one part of the original hypothesis the data does support.

Crypto.com's market-sizing reports give total owners (583m in 2023, 659m in 2024) and a Bitcoin-owner count for 2024 (337m). The “51% of crypto users own Bitcoin” figure used in earlier drafts is not a published statistic; it is those two numbers divided by each other, and it is labelled as such here.

Key Finding 6: Address- and account-based counts are the weakest data in this field — one person can hold several wallets, and exchange accounts bundle many people. We report them because they are the only adoption series available, not because they measure adoption.
